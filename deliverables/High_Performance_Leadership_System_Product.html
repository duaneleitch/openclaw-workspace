Module 1: The Cost of Unclear Leadership

Purpose: Create urgency without blaming managers. Teams do not become inconsistent because people lack effort alone. They become inconsistent when outcomes, ownership, feedback, and decision rights are fuzzy.

Teach: Separate activity, output, and outcome. “Attend more client meetings” is activity. “Send a follow-up” is output. “Every qualified opportunity has a tailored next step within one business day” is an outcome standard that can be observed and improved.

Facilitation flow (25 minutes): Open with a familiar friction: a customer waits, work returns for rework, or a decision travels through three people. Ask learners to locate the unclear element. Was the outcome unknown? Was the quality bar undefined? Was authority absent? Was follow-up missing?

Debrief: Do not accept “they need to be more accountable” as a diagnosis. Ask, “Accountable for what, by when, with what evidence, and with what authority?”

Watch-out: This is not an invitation to over-specify every task. The system clarifies the minimum needed for good judgment, then gives people room to act.

Case Study 1: The Aurora Service Desk

Situation. Aurora’s 14-person service desk has a 91% first-response target. The target has slipped to 76% for two months. The manager, Priya, reviews the queue twice daily and often reassigns tickets herself. Her team says they are “doing their best,” while customers report inconsistent updates.

What you can observe. A sample of 40 tickets shows that 18 lacked a next update date, 11 were assigned to specialists without a customer-facing owner, and 9 sat blocked for more than one day. Priya has not defined when agents can re-prioritize work or escalate resource conflicts. In meetings, she asks, “Can we all be more on top of this?”

New agent“I was waiting for the specialist.”No standard for proactive customer update or blocked-work escalation.
Customer“I can handle a delay; I cannot handle silence.”Quality includes communication, not just resolution.
Team task

Diagnose the primary gap. Draft one CLEAR standard for ticket ownership. Identify one warmth behavior Priya must use so that early escalation becomes safe rather than punitive.

Case Study 1 Debrief: From Rescue to Ownership

Diagnosis. Aurora does not principally have an effort problem. It has a standard, authority, and rhythm problem. Priya’s rescue behavior teaches agents that ownership ends at the queue boundary. Her vague request for people to be “more on top of it” gives no executable agreement.

CLEAR elementAurora agreement
Concrete outcomeEvery open customer ticket has a named customer-facing owner and a next update date.
Level of qualityCustomers receive an accurate update within one business day, even when another team owns the technical fix.
EvidenceQueue audit checks owner and next-update fields at 10:00 and 15:00; weekly sample checks message quality.
AuthorityAgents may re-prioritize within their tier and request specialist help; team lead resolves cross-tier conflict.
RhythmBlocked tickets are raised in the 15-minute daily risk review; unresolved blocks escalate after one business day.

Priya’s new opening

“We missed the response standard. I own the lack of clarity here. From today, each ticket has one customer owner and a next update date. What will make that difficult, and what authority do you need to make it work?”

What not to do

Do not announce a new rule, then continue privately reassigning tickets. That would keep the old system alive. Coach the owner in public routines; correct gaps privately and quickly.

Measure after 30 days: first response, percent of tickets with an update date, blocked-ticket age, repeat contacts, and agent clarity pulse.

Module 3: Create Commitment, Not Compliance

Managers need to set direction. Yet direction alone rarely creates ownership. Commitment comes when people understand the why, contribute their view of reality, know their authority, and can state the agreement in their own words.

StepManager moveWhy it matters
1. State outcome and why“We need accurate proposal handoffs because customers experience our teams as one company.”Connects the standard to purpose and customer impact.
2. Ask for current reality“What would make this difficult this week?”Surfaces constraints before they become excuses or surprises.
3. Agree standard and authority“You can choose the handoff format; legal exceptions come to me.”Creates practical autonomy.
4. Ask for a playback“How are you taking this forward?”Tests shared understanding without a quiz.
5. Set rhythm and support“What support do you need, and when will we review the first two?”Converts intention into a managed commitment.
Facilitator demonstration: Role-play a 90-second compliance conversation, then repeat it as a commitment conversation. Ask observers what changed in employee voice, risk discovery, and clarity.
Manager preparation

Module 4: Coach the Gap Early with FACT

Early correction is kinder and more effective than accumulated frustration. FACT keeps the manager out of labels and the employee out of defensiveness.

StepManager behaviorLanguage stem
F · FactsDescribe observable behavior and impact, not a character judgment.“Three client updates were sent after the agreed date.”
A · AskInvite the person’s perspective before assuming cause.“What got in the way?”
C · ClarifyRestate the standard and why it matters.“The standard is same-day updates because silence creates avoidable uncertainty.”
T · TestAgree next action, support, and check-in.“What will change this week, what support is needed, and when will we review?”

FACT is not soft

It is specific, timely, and follows through. Curiosity does not erase the standard. It helps a manager distinguish ambiguity, capability, capacity, or will before choosing the right intervention.

FACT is not a script

Use natural language. A manager who mechanically asks questions while avoiding the consequence still teaches that standards are optional.

Escalation logic: First confirm the agreement and remove barriers. If the same gap persists, state the impact, reset the corrective action, explain consequences consistent with policy, document the agreement, and follow up.

Accountability Without Drama

Accountability is an agreement plus a follow-through process. It is not a surprise, a personality judgment, a public performance, or a manager’s release valve for frustration.

SequenceWhat to doExample language
1. ReconfirmName the prior agreement.“Last Tuesday we agreed each account would have a next action by Friday.”
2. Describe resultState observed outcome and impact.“Six of twelve did not. That left the renewal team unable to plan.”
3. AskLearn what changed or was missed.“What happened between the agreement and Friday?”
4. CorrectSet action, support, and date.“Today we will complete the six; you will use the daily tracker through next Friday.”
5. Explain consequenceWhere appropriate, explain the next formal step consistent with policy.“If this repeats after the support we have agreed, we will move to our formal process.”
6. Document/follow upRecord the agreement and check it.“I will send the summary; we meet Wednesday at 10.”
Guardrails: Stay private, factual, timely, and proportionate. Distinguish performance management from conduct, and follow organizational HR and legal policy. Do not promise outcomes or impose consequences outside your authority.

The credible manager does not surprise people with standards, and does not abandon standards when the conversation gets uncomfortable.

Sample Dialogue: Firm, Fair, and Forward

Context: After two FACT conversations, Jonah again failed to complete a required quality check. His manager, Mei, must be direct without shaming.

Mei: “Jonah, we agreed on September 8 that every release would include the quality check before handoff. The last two releases went forward without it, and we found three defects after deployment. Help me understand what changed.”

Jonah: “The deadline moved and I thought getting it out was more important.”

Module 5: Delegate for Ownership

Delegation fails when managers hand over tasks but retain outcomes, decisions, and learning. Ownership grows when people understand the result, have meaningful control, receive appropriate guardrails, and can seek help without surrendering the work.

When participants say “we do not have time”: Acknowledge the constraint. Ask what rework, escalation, or manager rescue currently consumes. Position the rhythm as a replacement for low-value status traffic, not an additive bureaucracy.

When participants want scripts: Give language stems, then insist on natural phrasing. The manager must sound credible in their environment.

When someone describes a sensitive performance issue: Redirect to facts and process. Remind the group to follow local policy and seek HR support where required.

Inclusion practice: Avoid treating directness as one cultural style. Define directness as clear, respectful, evidence-based communication. Invite multiple ways to express warmth and challenge.

Energy recovery: After dense teaching, use pair rehearsals of 90 seconds each. Give observers one narrow criterion. Debrief patterns, not individual competence.

1. The Friday Escalation Cycle

Situation. A customer-support team has missed its same-day escalation target for three weeks. Priya notices only when a director forwards a Friday complaint. TermWorking definition
